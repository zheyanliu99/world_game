# Agentic Civilization Mode

This is a design note for a possible future mode. It does not replace the
current marble simulation. The main idea is to move the game from
physics-driven conquest to agent-driven strategy: the map stays, the kingdoms
stay, but each turn is decided by bounded choices made by faction agents.

## Core Idea

The current marble mode is exciting because many tiny units collide with the map
and produce emergent outcomes. The proposed mode would make those units
playable items instead of random balls.

In this mode, each kingdom is controlled by an agent. Each round, the agent sees
the current game state, predicts what rival kingdoms may do, then submits a
limited set of orders. The world engine validates those orders and resolves the
round.

The game would feel closer to a very simplified Civilization-style strategy
game, using Three Kingdoms as the default scenario:

- Cao Wei starts with stronger manpower, economy, and northern territory.
- Shu Han starts with better defensive geography and legitimacy pressure.
- Eastern Wu starts with river/naval advantages and a strong southern base.

The tension comes from planning, limited moves, imperfect information, and
resource pressure, not from random balls or sudden incidents.

## What Replaces Balls

Marbles can become visible, controllable units. A unit is still rendered on the
map, but it no longer bounces randomly. It has a location, owner, action state,
resources, and orders.

Possible unit types:

- Army: attacks, defends, patrols, escorts supply lines.
- Farmer: improves food output in owned territory.
- Worker: builds roads, forts, depots, and farms.
- Scout: reveals nearby enemy units, terrain, and resource flow.
- Supply caravan: transfers food, weapons, or gold between regions.
- Commander: improves nearby army actions, but is expensive and vulnerable.

The old visual language can remain useful. Many small circles still make the
map feel alive, but their motion represents decisions instead of randomness.

## Round Structure

Each round should have a clear loop:

1. World state is summarized.
2. Each faction agent receives an observation.
3. Each faction agent submits orders under a move budget.
4. The engine validates legal orders.
5. Orders resolve simultaneously.
6. Resources, morale, control, alliances, and visibility update.
7. A short narration/event summary is produced.

Simultaneous resolution matters. If Wei plans an attack on Jingzhou while Wu
secretly reinforces it, Wei's agent must reason about that possibility before
committing.

## Move Budget

Each general-led army and support unit can act once per round. Diplomacy remains
separately limited so a faction cannot spam treaties and betrayals in one turn.

Example costs:

- Rest unit: 1 AP
- Defend region: 1 AP
- Scout adjacent region: 1 AP
- Farm/improve region: 1 AP
- Transfer resources: 1 AP
- Move unit to adjacent owned region: 1 AP
- Attack adjacent enemy region: 2 AP
- Change national policy: 2 AP
- Propose alliance: 2 AP

Large kingdoms may receive more total actions, but the design should avoid
letting the largest kingdom snowball too easily. One option is to give more
actions for command capacity, not raw territory count.

## Resources

The game should separate at least a few resource pools:

- Food: sustains units, supports recovery, enables population growth.
- Weapons: needed for attacks and defensive readiness.
- Gold: supports diplomacy, logistics, upgrades, and emergency actions.
- Manpower: used to recruit armies, repair losses, and staff farms.
- Intel: generated by scouts; improves prediction and lowers surprise.

Food and weapons are especially important because they create real strategic
tradeoffs. A kingdom that attacks constantly may win territory but exhaust its
supply base.

## Unit Actions

Rest:
Recover readiness and morale. Useful after forced marches, failed attacks, or
starvation.

Attack:
Target an adjacent enemy region. Requires army readiness, weapons, supply, and a
legal war state. Attack strength depends on unit power, policy, terrain,
supporting units, morale, and supply.

Defend:
Increase local defensive score for the round. Forts, terrain, and war policy
can multiply this effect.

Scout:
Reveal enemy units, policy signals, supply routes, and likely next targets.
Scouting does not guarantee truth, but it improves the agent's observation.

Farm:
Improve food output in the region. Farming is slower than attacking but creates
long-term resilience.

Transfer:
Move food, weapons, or gold between regions. Transfer speed depends on roads,
distance, enemy raids, and national policy.

## National Policies

Each faction can set one national policy at a time in the first version. Later,
policies could be split into economy, military, and diplomacy slots.

Farming focus:
Increases food output and recovery. Improves transfer speed for food. Reduces
attack strength because labor and command attention are not concentrated on war.

War focus:
Improves attack and defense. Reduces food/gold output. Increases weapons usage
and morale loss if wars drag on.

Logistics focus:
Improves transfer speed, supply range, and reinforcement. Weaker direct combat
bonus than war focus, but better for multi-front empires.

Defense focus:
Improves forts, regional defense, and recovery after invasion. Reduces expansion
and slows attacks.

Diplomacy focus:
Improves alliance success chance, trade efficiency, and tribute value. Weakens
surprise attack options and may reduce military readiness.

Balanced:
No strong bonus or penalty. Good default for stable kingdoms.

## Alliances

Alliances should be real constraints, not just flavor.

An active alliance can:

- Prohibit attacks between allied kingdoms.
- Allow resource transfer between allied kingdoms.
- Share limited scouting information.
- Enable coordinated attacks against a third kingdom.
- Add betrayal cost if one side breaks the treaty.

Breaking an alliance should be possible, but costly. Costs might include
legitimacy loss, morale damage, temporary diplomatic isolation, or a warning
round before attacks become legal.

## Agent Observations

Each agent should not receive perfect world knowledge. It should receive a
structured observation like:

- Own regions, units, resources, policy, and alliances.
- Known enemy regions and visible units.
- Recent enemy actions that were observed.
- Border threats and supply problems.
- Scout reports with confidence levels.
- Public diplomatic messages.
- Victory progress and danger warnings.

The agent can also receive a memory summary from previous rounds. This lets it
form plans such as "Wu has been stockpiling weapons near Jingzhou, so defend
before expanding."

## Agent Output

Faction agents should submit machine-readable orders. A future engine could ask
Codex for JSON shaped like this:

```json
{
  "policy": "war_focus",
  "orders": [
    {
      "unit_id": "shu_army_01",
      "action": "defend",
      "region": "yizhou"
    },
    {
      "unit_id": "shu_scout_01",
      "action": "scout",
      "region": "jingzhou"
    },
    {
      "unit_id": "shu_caravan_01",
      "action": "transfer",
      "from_region": "yizhou",
      "to_region": "hanzhong",
      "resource": "weapons",
      "amount": 20
    }
  ],
  "diplomacy": [
    {
      "type": "propose_alliance",
      "target": "sun_quan",
      "duration_rounds": 6
    }
  ],
  "reasoning_summary": "Wei is likely to push south, so Shu defends Hanzhong while trying to keep Wu neutral."
}
```

The engine should validate this output strictly. Illegal attacks, impossible
transfers, overspending, or duplicate unit orders should be rejected or converted
into idle actions.

## Combat Resolution

Combat can be mostly deterministic with small uncertainty, or fully
deterministic if the design wants maximum explainability.

Attack score can use:

- Army strength
- Weapons supply
- Readiness
- Morale
- Commander support
- War policy bonus
- Terrain penalty
- Enemy defense order
- Fort level
- Supply distance

Defense score can use:

- Garrison strength
- Region terrain
- Food supply
- Fort level
- Defense policy bonus
- Nearby allied support
- Local morale

The result should not usually flip a full province instantly. Better outcomes:

- Attacker loses and retreats.
- Defender holds but loses readiness.
- Attacker establishes contested control.
- Attacker captures the region after sustained pressure.
- Defender collapses if morale, food, and manpower are already low.

This makes campaigns feel like campaigns, not single dice rolls.

## Territory Control

Territory can have more states than simple owner/not owner:

- Owned
- Allied access
- Contested
- Occupied
- Under siege
- Recently captured

Recently captured regions should produce less food and gold until stabilized.
This keeps conquest from becoming instantly profitable.

## Three Kingdoms Example Round

Round 12, late 210s:

- Wei predicts Shu wants Hanzhong and Wu wants to wait.
- Shu predicts Wei will reinforce the north and Wu may accept alliance.
- Wu predicts Wei and Shu will exhaust each other, so it farms and scouts.

Possible orders:

- Wei chooses war focus, reinforces Hanzhong, scouts Jingzhou, transfers weapons
  from the north.
- Shu chooses logistics focus, defends Yizhou, scouts Hanzhong, proposes an
  alliance with Wu.
- Wu chooses farming focus, improves Jiangdong, scouts Jingzhou, avoids fighting
  either side.

Resolution:

- Wei's attack is delayed by poor supply.
- Shu gains intel and prepares a counterattack.
- Wu gains food and sees both northern armies moving.
- The next round becomes a diplomatic problem, not just a battle problem.

## Why This Could Be Better

This design gives the game an actual strategic mind. The interesting question is
no longer "which ball bounced into the right province?" It becomes "what did
each ruler think the others would do, and did they commit to the right plan?"

It also makes historical flavor more meaningful. A Three Kingdoms story is full
of campaigns, alliances, betrayals, food problems, river defense, legitimacy,
and bad guesses. Agents are a much better fit for that than pure random motion.

## Risks

The biggest risk is pacing. If every round is too complex, the simulation may
feel like reading a spreadsheet. The visual layer needs to stay alive: units
move, supply lines draw, scouts reveal fog, battles animate, and policy changes
show clear effects.

Another risk is agent inconsistency. Agents need strict schemas, concise
observations, and validation. The game should not trust free-form text as game
state.

Snowballing is also a risk. Policies, supply limits, recently captured unrest,
and command capacity can slow runaway conquest.

## Possible Implementation Path

Phase 1: Design only
Capture rules, unit types, resources, policies, agent inputs, and order schema.

Phase 2: Non-visual prototype
Add a turn-based simulator that runs on the existing region map and prints round
summaries. Use simple scripted agents before Codex agents.

Phase 3: Agent integration
Give each faction a structured observation and require JSON orders. Add strict
validation and fallback idle orders.

Phase 4: Visual mode
Render units, region states, supply lines, policy markers, scouting range, and
campaign summaries on the existing map.

Phase 5: Scenario authoring
Create Three Kingdoms starting states with faction personalities, starting
resources, commanders, and historical pressure.

## First Minimal Ruleset

For a very small first version:

- Three factions: Wei, Shu, Wu.
- Three resources: food, weapons, gold.
- Four unit types: army, worker, scout, caravan.
- Six actions: rest, attack, defend, scout, farm, transfer.
- Four policies: balanced, farming, war, logistics.
- One action per unit or general per round, with idle units defaulting to defense.
- Alliances prohibit attacks and allow transfers.
- Combat resolves at city level, while 州 control remains a derived summary.
- Agents submit JSON only.

That is enough to test whether the core idea is fun before building a larger
Civilization-like system.

## Demo Implementation Defaults

The first web demo should use a separate agentic mode and leave Marble mode
unchanged. The player controls 蜀汉, while 曹魏 and 东吴 are controlled by an
agent provider. Tests and offline play use deterministic mock agents; a live
OpenAI-backed provider can be used when credentials are configured.

The demo is manually paced. It uses 100 rounds as a pacing marker, but the game
does not award dominance or score victories; a faction wins only by eliminating
all rival factions. The player advances each round from the web UI after editing
a strategy command, selecting a policy, and optionally adding structured unit or
diplomacy orders.

The Shu UI includes a deterministic advisor recommendation each round. It
suggests a policy, one legal action per available unit, optional diplomacy, and a
short rationale; applying the recommendation writes normal structured orders.

The v1 web stack is FastAPI plus static HTML/CSS/JS canvas rendering. Unit
orders are stored as structured JSON, while the strategy text is saved as agent
context and used for simple player-plan assistance when no explicit orders are
selected.

The web map currently uses the reusable China real-map cache at
`data/maps/sanguo_real_map_prepared.json`, generated from the
`configs/maps/sanguo_real_map.json` source config. City ownership, stacks, and
combat effects remain city-level overlays on top of that familiar China map.
Roads still drive movement and supply costs in the simulation, but the browser
does not draw road lines by default so the campaign view stays readable.
The city graph uses the same 1280x720 coordinate system as the current China
map cache. Any future map swap must either update `configs/maps/sanguo_cities.json`
to the new canvas size or add an explicit coordinate transform before the UI is
considered valid.

Route flavor and city placement were checked against public Three Kingdoms
map references such as Kongming's AD262 map page, IKCEST's AD262 description,
CHGIS historical GIS downloads, Academia Sinica's Sanguo WMTS metadata, and
Qinling/Shu Roads notes. These are used as references for city placement,
broad historical regions, and route types only.

The current demo has moved to a curated city graph. Generals lead named armies
with soldier counts and local portrait assets; cities own income, supply,
attacks, defense coverage, retreat, surrender, and animations. 州 control is
derived from city ownership for ranking and map tinting.

The city graph now contains 48 curated cities, enough to give every displayed
州 at least one city and to make control changes happen visibly at the city
level. The real-map renderer keeps the broad 州 summary, but city nodes and
local control influence are colored directly from `city_owners`; road lines are
kept hidden in the UI.

Cities are connected by explicit symmetric `roads`. Neighbors are derived from
the road table instead of freeform city lists. Each road stores route type,
distance, food cost, gold cost, soldier attrition, readiness cost, and a source
note. Movement is one adjacent road per round, so 成都 can reach 武都 only by
future-route planning through 汉中 or 褒郡, not by a direct order.

`move`, `attack`, `scout`, `transfer`, `reinforce`, and retreat all use the
same travel-cost function. Normal travel requires enough food and gold from the
source city or faction pool; forced retreat can continue when short on supply
but doubles attrition and fatigue.

The map UI aggregates same-city armies into faction stacks. Each stack chooses
a lead general by historical fame and office priority, then renders one scalable
token with the leader portrait/name, general count, and total soldiers. This
keeps crowded cities readable while preserving the importance of figures such
as 诸葛亮, 关羽, 张飞, 赵云, 曹操, 司马懿, 周瑜, and 陆逊.

Battles are active campaigns rather than instant incidents. An attack creates a
city battle lasting 2-10 rounds; both sides can reinforce from adjacent cities,
and committed armies can retreat. Final battle results still use deterministic
weighted randomness with soldier count, general stats, readiness, policy,
terrain, fortification, and supply as factors.

After every round and capture, stranded units are cleaned up. A general or unit
standing in an enemy city without an active battle, retreat path, or alliance
must retreat, surrender, or be destroyed, so armies cannot linger indefinitely
inside hostile territory.

Resource transfer is implemented through city supply and summarized by region.
Caravans move pooled food, weapons, or gold into a target city; local supply then
improves battle and recovery outcomes there.

Attacking, scouting, and transfers spend food. Armies require food and gold for
upkeep, while gold plus manpower can reinforce depleted units. Moving gold into
a front city also acts as battle pay, improving local attack and defense.

City conquest now awards most stored food, weapons, and gold in the captured
city plus manpower drawn from the local population, while battle damage reduces
the city's development and population.

General metadata lives in local seed files under `data/generals/`, with a
BigQuery-compatible schema and optional loader script documented in
`docs/bigquery_general_seed.md`. The app does not create cloud projects or
require GCP credentials.

The local general seed database currently contains 98 curated historical
generals: 38 for 魏, 30 for 蜀, and 30 for 吴. A new game deploys a larger
initial subset while keeping the rest as a discoverable pool. Each round,
factions may discover a new general based on owned population and how depleted
their active general roster is.

Random incidents are deterministic by game round and can affect a city or area:
weather, drought, flood, banditry, harvests, volunteers, armory finds, and trade
booms can change food, gold, weapons, manpower, population, soldiers, or
development. These events are exposed in API state and rendered as map/log
events.

The web UI is localized for Chinese gameplay. 军师建议 is generated for every
movable object, uses Chinese unit/general labels, prioritizes the player's
strategy text when legal, and can propose ten-round alliances with either 魏 or
吴. Target factions may accept or object through deterministic weighted
diplomacy logic.

For future UI work, browser QA is required for any map, layout, Phaser/canvas,
coordinate, avatar, or command-panel change. The acceptance check must include
a fresh local browser reload, a screenshot, and a quick visual confirmation that
city nodes, general stacks, labels, and animations align with the current map.
